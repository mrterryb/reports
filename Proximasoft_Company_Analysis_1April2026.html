Executive Summary

A 25-year-old Mauritius ICT firm with an exceptional gross margin and genuine regional reach — but a balance sheet so thin on equity that one bad quarter could trigger a liquidity crisis.

ProximaSoft Ltd (File No. C25647, BRN C07025647) was incorporated on 10 October 2000 under the former name G2D Development Services Co Ltd, and has traded from The Cubicle, 2nd Floor, 35-37 Royal Road, Phoenix for over five years. The company operates three declared business lines: Computer/ICT Related Activities, Distribution of General Merchandise, and Training Institution. Its primary commercial identity is as a B2B ICT services and software company, offering the Solis ERP suite, the ProximaCloud managed hosting platform, IT infrastructure managed services, and custom software development using WinDev/WebDev. Regional markets include Reunion Island and Madagascar.

The company is 100% owned by Business Boosters Investments Ltd (BBI Group), a Mauritian holding entity (Reg. No. C076899) whose Group Chief Executive Officer is Jean Philippe Marrier d'Unienville — the same individual who has served as ProximaSoft's sole director since its founding in October 2000. This means ProximaSoft is effectively a wholly-owned operating subsidiary within the BBI Group ecosystem, with the founder occupying both the operational leadership of ProximaSoft and the group holding company simultaneously. This concentration of authority in a single person is the defining structural characteristic of the business.

For the financial year ended 30 September 2024, ProximaSoft reported revenue of MUR 38.8M, a spectacular gross profit margin of 87.9%, and a net profit of MUR 4.0M (10.3% margin). These are strong operational signals. The critical counterweight is the balance sheet: total equity stands at just MUR 1.39M against total liabilities of MUR 21.15M — a debt-to-equity ratio of 15.2×. The bulk of current liabilities (MUR 9.1M in trade payables and MUR 6.4M in short-term borrowings) dwarfs the equity cushion. The EOS suitability score of 6.4/10 reflects genuine readiness signals — primarily the growth complexity, founder-centric decision-making, and multi-product sprawl — balanced against leadership depth constraints.

Key Finding — The Profitable Prisoner of Its Own Structure
ProximaSoft is operationally excellent — its 87.9% gross margin is best-in-class for a Mauritian ICT firm and its 10.3% net margin is solid. The business is genuinely good at what it does. The strategic problem is structural: a single director who is simultaneously running the parent holding company, a balance sheet funded almost entirely by payables and short-term debt rather than equity, and three business lines competing for management attention without a clear prioritisation framework. This is the classic EOS entry scenario: a profitable business that is growing in spite of its structure, not because of it. The conversation opener is not "you are struggling" — it is "imagine what this business does when its founder is free to lead instead of operate."

The 2 Numbers That Matter Most
1. Debt-to-Equity: 15.2× (benchmark <2×). The equity base of MUR 1.39M is so thin that the company is effectively operating on creditor goodwill. If BBI Group's intra-group balances were restructured or withdrawn, ProximaSoft would face an immediate balance sheet crisis. The fix requires either retained earnings accumulation (halting distributions) or a formal equity injection. At the FY2024 PAT of MUR 4.01M, retaining earnings for two years without distributions would more than treble the equity base and bring D/E down to approximately 4×.

2. DPO: 124 days (benchmark 30–60 days). The company owes MUR 9.06M to trade creditors and is paying them in approximately four months. This is the structural funding mechanism — but it is fragile. If three or four key suppliers simultaneously tightened terms to 60 days, ProximaSoft would need to find MUR 4–5M in additional short-term funding almost immediately. Improving collections (DSO) and renegotiating formal credit facilities would reduce this exposure.

Structural Competitive Advantages

1. Local-first proprietary ERP with 25-year compliance depth. Solis is built specifically for Mauritius regulatory and accounting requirements (MRA compliance, MUR currency, local labour law). This is genuinely hard to replicate quickly — it represents decades of embedded domain knowledge. Global competitors like Odoo or Sage require significant localisation investment to match this.

2. Bundled product-service stickiness. Clients who adopt Solis ERP typically also take managed IT services and ProximaCloud hosting — creating a multi-product relationship that is costly and disruptive to exit. This recurring revenue bundle (software + infrastructure + support) is the most defensible part of the business model and a key driver of the 87.9% gross margin.

3. Sub-regional positioning in underserved markets. Active presence in Reunion Island and Madagascar gives ProximaSoft a genuine first-mover advantage in markets where local Mauritius-built ICT solutions are rare. This geographic spread reduces dependency on the Mauritius domestic market alone.

Industry Trends and Tailwinds

  • Mauritius ICT market growing at 5.4% CAGR to USD 203.9M by 2030 — structural tailwind for all ICT service providers. Government digitisation programmes (Smart Mauritius, Digital Economy Strategy) are creating demand for ERP adoption among SMEs that have historically operated manually.
  • SME ERP adoption accelerating as post-pandemic operational pressures drive smaller businesses toward digitalisation. ProximaSoft's affordable, locally-hosted Solis suite is well-positioned to capture this demand — particularly against expensive global ERPs with long implementation timelines.
  • Data sovereignty concerns favour local cloud providers. Mauritius financial services and healthcare sectors face increasing regulatory pressure around data localisation — creating demand for ProximaCloud's on-island hosting proposition over AWS/Azure deployments that route data internationally.
  • Cybersecurity demand rising sharply. ProximaSoft's firewall, antivirus, and backup services are benefiting from increased corporate awareness of cyber risk following high-profile incidents in the Indian Ocean region. This is a growth vector within the existing managed services bundle.
  • AI and automation integration risk. Global ERP vendors (Microsoft Dynamics, SAP, Odoo) are integrating AI features at speed. ProximaSoft's WinDev/WebDev-based platform will need continuous investment to remain competitive with AI-assisted features — a risk for a small team without a dedicated R&D budget.
  • Talent competition intensifying in Mauritius ICT. The island's positioning as an Africa-facing tech hub (Accenture, Concentrix, Allianz presence) is creating wage pressure for ICT professionals — a headwind for a 24-person company competing for the same talent as multinationals.
Marketing & Digital Presence Gap

EOS Suitability Assessment

EOS is most effective in private companies of 10–250 people experiencing growth complexity — disconnect between vision, execution, and accountability. Each dimension rated 1–10 with evidence from public data and financial analysis.

01 — Organisational Size and Complexity 7 / 10

At approximately 24 employees, ProximaSoft sits precisely in the EOS sweet spot of 10–250 people. The company operates three declared business lines (ICT services, general merchandise, training), a proprietary ERP product, a cloud platform, a sub-regional presence across three markets (Mauritius, Reunion, Madagascar), and an intra-group relationship with BBI Group. This is considerable complexity for a 24-person firm — exactly the kind of multi-threaded operation that benefits most from EOS structure. The company is not too small to justify the investment, and not so large that cultural change becomes unwieldy.

02 — Leadership Openness 5 / 10

Jean Philippe Marrier d'Unienville is an entrepreneurial founder who has built a 25-year business and simultaneously leads a holding group — signals of strategic thinking and willingness to grow. However, a sole director structure maintained for 25 years with no co-directors or named management layer suggests a strong preference for personal control. Founders who have never operated with a formal leadership team sometimes find EOS disciplines (accountability, transparency, letting go) personally uncomfortable. Score is neutral pending a direct conversation — the BBI Group role suggests he thinks in terms of systems, which is an encouraging signal.

03 — Growth Stage and Pain Points 8 / 10

MUR 38.8M revenue, three business segments, active regional expansion, a proprietary product line, and a parallel holding company leadership role — this is a company at the precise growth inflection point where EOS delivers maximum value. The financial data reveals the classic pain symptoms: admin costs running at 68.6% of revenue with no visible management overhead suggests the founder is still doing operational work that should be delegated. DSO of 64 days and DPO of 124 days indicate no systematic process ownership for collections and creditor management. These are textbook EOS entry pain points.

04 — Current Systems and Processes 7 / 10

There is a productive irony in a company that builds ERP and process management tools for its clients while its own balance sheet shows the hallmarks of informal internal management: a 124-day DPO, a 64-day DSO, and an equity base that implies profits have not been systematically retained according to a capital plan. ProximaSoft almost certainly has good technical processes (its product quality and 25-year track record confirm this) but likely lacks documented operational processes for non-technical functions: sales pipeline, credit control, cash management, and people management. This gap is exactly what EOS process documentation addresses.

05 — Company Culture 5 / 10

Twenty-five years of continuous operation with a stable founder suggests a cohesive culture — likely values-driven around technical excellence and client service. However, founder-centric cultures can be resistant to the accountability and transparency disciplines EOS introduces. No public signals of staff turnover, culture initiatives, or team development programmes are visible. The score is neutral — the culture may be a facilitator or a barrier to EOS adoption, and this can only be assessed in a direct conversation with Jean Philippe.

06 — Decision-Making Patterns 8 / 10

This is the highest-confidence dimension. A sole director who has held uninterrupted authority since October 2000, simultaneously leads the parent holding company, and has not appointed a co-director or deputy in 25 years is — by definition — a centralised decision-maker. Every strategic and operational decision passes through one person. This creates a classic EOS bottleneck: the founder is the constraint on the company's growth, not the market. The Accountability Chart and Delegate & Elevate tools would have immediate and measurable impact for this company.

07 — Vision Clarity 6 / 10

Three declared business lines — ICT services, general merchandise, and training — without an obvious strategic narrative connecting them suggests that growth has been opportunistic rather than vision-directed. The combination of a proprietary ERP product, a managed cloud platform, sub-regional expansion, and an e-wallet product (ProxiPass) is either a coherent ecosystem play or a collection of disconnected opportunities — and this distinction matters enormously for what the next five years should look like. A V/TO exercise would force the clarity that 25 years of organic growth has not produced.
