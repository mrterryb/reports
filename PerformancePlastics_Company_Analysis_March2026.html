Executive Summary

A profitable, established Mauritian plastics manufacturer with genuine scale and diversification — but with working-capital drag and classic signs that more structure would unlock the next level.

Performance Plastics Ltd is a domestic Mauritian private company incorporated on 14 September 1990 and operating from the Industrial Zone, Riche Terre. Public information points to two main activity clusters: packaging manufacturing and a later-added equipment division serving packaging and food-processing customers. Its website positions the company as a long-standing local manufacturer with reach across Mauritius and into parts of Africa.

The share register visible in the CBRIS extract is concentrated: Lim Sam Kwet How is listed as the sole shareholder with 2,259 ordinary shares. Governance appears lean: one registered director, one corporate secretary, and an external auditor. That usually means faster decisions, but it also raises the old-owner-business question: how much of the company still runs through one person?

Financially, this is not a distressed company. FY2024 revenue was Rs 299.7m and net profit after tax was Rs 63.1m. Liquidity is extremely strong on paper, borrowings are nil, and the balance sheet is equity-heavy. The catch is receivables: debtor days sit at roughly 122, which is far too lazy for a business that should be converting good profits into even stronger cash velocity. EOS relevance is not about rescue here; it is about sharpening control, speed, accountability, and succession-readiness.

Key Finding — Strong Business, Probably Under-Managed Relative to Its Potential
Performance Plastics looks like the kind of company that can keep winning without EOS — right up until complexity, people dependency, or growth friction starts biting. The hard data says the business is financially solid. The softer public signals say it has grown, diversified, and reached meaningful operational scale. That combination usually creates a dangerous illusion: “we're doing fine, so the system is fine.” It probably isn't. The real opportunity is not turnaround. It is installing enough leadership cadence, role clarity, and measurable accountability to shorten cash cycles, reduce founder/key-person dependence, and make the next phase of growth less heroic and less personality-driven.

At a Glance

Commercial Strengths

Established market position in packaging, a clear biodegradable-product story aligned with Mauritian regulation, a diversification leg through equipment, ISO quality positioning, and evidence of service-led customer relationships across multiple sectors including retail, hospitality, and agriculture.

Financial Vulnerabilities

Receivables are the obvious weak spot. Roughly Rs 99.9m is tied up in trade and other receivables. Inventory is also sizeable at Rs 97.6m. This is not a liquidity crisis today, but it is exactly how profitable businesses quietly suffocate their own agility.

EOS Readiness Signal

Size-plus-diversification is the signal: manufacturing, equipment sales, roughly 100 employees, one site, multi-segment customers, and a concentrated leadership structure. That is prime territory for accountability-chart confusion, issue pile-up, and inconsistent meeting discipline unless someone has already built a real operating system.

Company Overview

A long-established owner-led manufacturer that has evolved from plastic bags into a broader packaging-and-equipment business.

Founding and Corporate Record

PERFORMANCE PLASTICS LTD was incorporated on 14 September 1990 as a private domestic company under file number C9035 and business registration number C07009035. The company is listed as Live. The registered office is Industrial Zone, Riche Terre. The CBRIS extract shows stated capital of Rs 225,900 across 2,259 ordinary shares with no unpaid amount. Minutarial Ltd is listed as company secretary and Chartist Consultant – Chartered Certified Accountant as auditor.

Annual returns appear up to date for the last three years shown, and financial summaries have been filed for FY2022, FY2023, and FY2024. That is a small but useful governance signal: this is not a sloppy registry file. Public-facing company history states the business began in a 100 m² space in Port Louis, later moved to Le Hochet, then into its own 3,600 m² facility at Riche Terre. In 2018 it launched the Performance Equipment division as a diversification step.

Strategic Note — Ownership Simplicity Cuts Both Ways
A concentrated share register and lean governance often make businesses more entrepreneurial. Great. It also means decisions, relationships, and institutional memory can become sticky around one person. For EOS conversations, that is usually gold: not because the company is broken, but because scale starts punishing informality.

Leadership and Directors

Lim Sam Kwet How — Director

Appointed 14 September 1990. Service address shown as Pamplemousses, Mauritius. Also listed as sole shareholder in the available CBRIS extract. Functionally, this looks like founder-owner leadership, even if the public sources do not spell out the exact executive title.

Minutarial Ltd — Secretary

Appointed 9 January 2020. Corporate secretarial support based in Beau Bassin. The presence of a professional company secretary suggests basic governance housekeeping is being handled properly.

Public Management Signal

LinkedIn indicates approximately 100 employees and shows a small visible employee footprint online. That implies the business is material enough to require a real leadership layer, even if the public corporate registry only shows one registered director.

Products and Services Portfolio

The operating model appears split into two clusters. First: packaging manufacturing, including biodegradable bags, garbage bags, bubble wrap, films, paper bags, potting bags, rolls, and custom-made packaging. Second: equipment distribution under the Performance Equipment division, covering food-processing and packaging equipment such as mixers, ovens, and sealing machines. That mix matters. It means this is not just a narrow converter or bag producer anymore; it is using adjacent commercial capabilities to widen wallet share and smooth customer relevance.

Geographic Presence

The operating site and registered office are both at Riche Terre, Mauritius. Public website content says the company serves all areas of Mauritius and some regions of Africa. LinkedIn states it delivers across Mauritius and certain African regions and serves customer sectors ranging from hospitality to retail and agriculture.

Digital Presence
Unlike plenty of Mauritian mid-market businesses that still look half-invisible online, Performance Plastics has a functioning website, visible product pages, direct contact details, and an active LinkedIn presence. That is not revolutionary. It is just refreshingly competent.

Charge Register

The file shows several historical floating charges over movable and immovable property: Rs 9.0m charges dated 8 August 2000 (appearing twice in the extract), Rs 1.65m dated 23 March 2001, Rs 1.5m dated 26 September 2001, and Rs 0.4m dated 18 April 2005. The pattern suggests the company used secured financing in earlier growth years but currently reports no borrowings in the FY2024 summary. That is a nice arc: fund growth, then clean up the balance sheet.

Working-Capital Interpretation
With no reported borrowings and only trivial finance cost, the company is not being squeezed by banks. It is potentially squeezing itself by carrying too much inventory and allowing receivables to age too slowly. That is a management problem, not a capital-structure problem.

Structural Competitive Advantages

First, the company appears to have a real local operating footprint rather than a thin trading shell: a 3,600 m² facility, manufacturing capability, and broad local delivery presence. Second, it adapted early to Mauritius’s plastic-bag regulatory shift and positioned itself around biodegradable products, which gives it more credibility than late followers. Third, it has widened its relevance through equipment sales, which can deepen customer relationships and reduce dependence on a single product logic. None of this is invincible, but it is defensible enough to matter.

Industry Trends and Tailwinds

  • Manufacturing remains an important contributor to the Mauritian economy, which supports industrial relevance but also means competition for efficiency is real, not theoretical.
  • Environmental regulation creates pressure on conventional plastics while rewarding firms that moved early into biodegradable or compliant alternatives.
  • Customers increasingly expect suppliers to provide not just products, but practical problem-solving and dependable lead times.
  • Working-capital pressure is a silent killer in import- and inventory-heavy island economies; businesses with slow collections get punished fast when conditions tighten.
  • Diversification into equipment can strengthen relationships, but it also introduces extra complexity in pricing, support, and accountability.
  • A competent website and digital presence are now basic hygiene, not competitive magic. The winners use digital visibility to reinforce trust, speed, and professionalism.
Competitive Gap to Watch
The website is decent, but public evidence of leadership depth, strategic messaging, case studies, or structured thought leadership is thin. For a company of this apparent scale, that is not fatal. It does, however, suggest the commercial brand may still rely more on legacy relationships and operational reputation than on a deliberate growth engine.

EOS Suitability Assessment

EOS is most effective in private companies of 10–250 people experiencing growth complexity — disconnect between vision, execution, and accountability. Each dimension rated 1–10 with evidence from public data and financial analysis.

01 — Organisational Size and Complexity 8 / 10

LinkedIn indicates roughly 100 employees and the business spans manufacturing, product breadth, and an equipment division. That is comfortably in the EOS sweet spot. Big enough for complexity, small enough that leadership behaviour still sets the tone.

02 — Leadership Openness 6 / 10

Public clues are mixed. Governance filings are current, the company adapted to regulation, and it diversified commercially. Those are positive signals. But concentrated ownership and thin public visibility into leadership depth make it impossible to assume openness to accountability and transparency without a real conversation.

03 — Growth Stage and Pain Points 8 / 10

This looks like a business beyond startup improvisation but still exposed to mid-stage friction: working-capital drag, diversification complexity, likely key-person dependence, and the managerial demands of running multiple commercial engines. Classic EOS territory.

04 — Current Systems and Processes 6 / 10

ISO certification and sustained operational performance suggest processes exist. Good. But strong process pockets do not equal a full company operating system. The receivables and inventory signals suggest there is still plenty of management friction somewhere in the machine.

05 — Company Culture 6 / 10

Public messaging emphasises value, service, quality, honesty, and reliability. That is encouraging, but it is still brochure language until tested. No obvious public red flags, but no hard evidence of deeply institutionalised accountability culture either.

06 — Decision-Making Patterns 7 / 10

One visible shareholder-director and a lean governance structure suggest decisions are probably centralised. That can be fast, but it often becomes a ceiling. EOS can help convert founder velocity into distributed clarity instead of central bottlenecking.

07 — Vision Clarity 6 / 10

There is evidence of mission language, diversification intent, and practical market focus. What is missing publicly is the sharper stuff: where the company is going, what it will refuse to be, and how the divisions fit into one coherent strategic direction.

08 — Meeting Culture 4 / 10

There is no public evidence of a structured meeting rhythm, scorecard discipline, or issue-solving cadence. That does not prove the absence of discipline, but it usually means the company is not deliberately using meetings as a management weapon. Most aren't.

EOS Suitability

Overall Rating: High-Moderate — Strong Candidate If Leadership Wants the Next Level

Performance Plastics looks like a strong EOS candidate because it is old enough, large enough, and commercially broad enough for complexity to matter. The gating factor is not financial distress. It is leadership appetite. If the owner and senior team want more control, faster decisions, cleaner accountability, and better cash velocity, EOS fits. If they are content running on experience and informal coordination, they will keep leaving money and speed on the table.

Highest-Priority EOS Tools for Performance Plastics

Scorecard & Measurables

The business needs a real weekly scorecard around cash conversion, debtor days, overdue receivables, aged inventory, on-time delivery, machine uptime, and gross margin by product family. Right now the balance sheet is politely hinting that not enough is being watched weekly.

Accountability Chart

Manufacturing plus equipment plus likely owner-centralised decisions is exactly how seat confusion creeps in. A proper Accountability Chart would separate sales, operations, finance, supply chain, and divisional responsibility instead of letting everything blur into “management”.

Level 10 Meeting

If leadership meetings are currently reactive, a disciplined weekly L10 would surface issues faster and stop operational noise from swallowing strategic work. This is especially important when working-capital discipline needs cross-functional ownership.

Issues List & IDS

Receivables, stock turns, customer terms, and division coordination are not spreadsheet problems first. They are issue-solving problems. IDS would force the team to stop circling symptoms and actually solve root causes.

V/TO

The company has enough history and breadth that it likely needs sharper strategic alignment: what role does equipment really play, what markets matter most, and what must the business look like in three years for diversification to be worth it?

Rocks

This company should not launch EOS with vague improvement intentions. It needs 90-day Rocks tied to collections, stock rationalisation, divisional accountability, and leadership cadence. Otherwise the whole thing becomes motivational wallpaper.

Recommendations

Eight recommendations in total: four financial/operational and four EOS-specific. The sequencing principle is simple — tighten the financial plumbing first, then use EOS to make the improvements stick instead of sliding back into habit.

Financial and Operational Recommendations

Recommendation 01

Run a 13-week receivables and cash-conversion sprint

Assign Finance to produce a customer-level aged-debt pack within two weeks, then review it weekly with Sales and Operations. Segment debtors into strategic accounts, slow payers, and problem accounts. Success means cutting DSO by at least 20 days within one quarter, which would release roughly Rs 16m+ of cash. That is not theory. That is speed.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 02

Attack inventory by SKU family, not by vague stock clean-up talk

Operations and Supply Chain should classify stock into fast, medium, slow, and obsolete, then define reorder logic and minimum-turn expectations by family. Success means lifting inventory turnover above 3.0× within six months and freeing dead capital without damaging service levels.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 03

Separate manufacturing economics from equipment economics

Finance should split reporting by the packaging and equipment activities. Gross margin, EBIT contribution, working-capital usage, and customer concentration should be visible by engine. Success means management can answer, without hand-waving, which division is truly creating value and which one is merely busy.

Within 30–60 Days
Recommendation 04

Document customer terms discipline and escalation rules

Commercial leadership should stop treating payment terms as a relationship free-for-all. Define who can approve terms, when orders are blocked, and how overdue accounts are escalated. Success means fewer heroic exceptions and cleaner cash behaviour from the customer base.

Within 30–60 Days

EOS Implementation Recommendations

Recommendation 05

Start with a Focus Day built around reality, not EOS theatre

Bring the owner and true leadership team into one room and surface the real pain: receivables, inventory, divisional complexity, people dependency, and decision bottlenecks. Success means the team leaves aligned on the top handful of issues and agrees that the status quo has an actual cost.

Within 90 Days / During EOS
Recommendation 06

Draft an Accountability Chart hypothesis before doing org-chart cosmetics

Build a first-pass leadership Accountability Chart with clear seats for Visionary/Integrator logic, Sales, Operations, Finance, Supply Chain/Procurement, and possibly a divisional owner for Equipment. Success means each major function has one throat to choke and one person who genuinely owns results.

Within 90 Days / During EOS
Recommendation 07

Make the first 90-day Rock about cash, not inspiration

The first quarter of EOS should include a Rock such as “Reduce DSO from 122 days to below 100 days by quarter-end” with milestones by ageing-bucket cleanup, customer-term reset, and weekly follow-up discipline. Success means leadership sees EOS producing a hard financial win quickly.

Within 90 Days / During EOS
Recommendation 08

Use the V/TO to answer one brutal strategic question

What exactly is Performance Plastics becoming: a disciplined packaging manufacturer with adjacent equipment capability, or a broader industrial solutions business? That sounds philosophical. It is not. It determines talent, structure, focus, and where future complexity should or should not be allowed.

Within 90 Days / During EOS

Overall Assessment — Conclusion

The Bottom Line

This is a good business. Possibly a very good one. Profitable, established, asset-backed, debt-light, commercially relevant. The real opportunity is not saving it. It is preventing a solid owner-led business from capping itself through slow cash cycles, fuzzy accountabilities, and growth complexity that quietly outruns management discipline.

The Recommended Sequence

  1. 01Within 2 weeks: produce aged receivables and inventory stratification with named accountabilities.
  2. 02Within 30 days: align leadership around the real issues in a Focus Day.
  3. 03Within 45 days: finalise a first-pass Accountability Chart and weekly scorecard.
  4. 04Within the first EOS quarter: run a Rock to materially reduce DSO and improve stock turns.
  5. 05Within 90 days: clarify the strategic role of the equipment division inside the V/TO.
