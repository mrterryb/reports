Executive Summary

CNOI Investissements Ltd is a small marine-leisure subsidiary inside the wider CNOI / IBL ecosystem, but its own filed numbers point to a thinly profitable trading operation sitting on a badly stretched balance sheet.

The legal entity reviewed here is CNOI Investissements Ltd, incorporated on 23 May 2016 as a Mauritian domestic private company. Its stated activity is the renting of pleasure boats with crew or fishing cruises, with its principal place of business in Freeport Zone 11, Mer Rouge, Port Louis. The company is fully owned by Chantier Naval de l'Océan Indien Limited.

That ownership link matters. CNOI Investissements is not a stand-alone entrepreneurial business in the wild; it sits within a larger industrial marine group controlled through CNOI and ultimately connected to IBL. That gives it strategic shelter, operational adjacency, and likely access to group infrastructure. It also means decision-making may be slower, more centralised, and more political than in a founder-led SME.

Financially, the picture is mixed. Gross margin is decent at 34.4%, so the core service model is not obviously broken. But the company ends FY2025 with only 3.8% net margin, a current ratio of just 0.18x, and negative equity of MUR 9,167,075. That is ugly. The business appears to be surviving off creditor funding and group support rather than on balance-sheet strength.

Key Finding — weak balance sheet, not necessarily weak gross economics
The most important signal is not revenue, and not even profit. It is the combination of negative equity, very low liquidity, and extreme creditor days. In plain English: this entity may still be able to trade, but it is not carrying its own weight financially. If you were approaching this company from an EOS angle, the conversation should be less about generic growth and more about whether leadership has real clarity on purpose, accountability, and the economic logic of keeping this vehicle structured as it is.

Structural Competitive Advantages

The defensible advantages are not flashy. They are likely: access to marine assets and technical maintenance support through the wider CNOI platform; group governance and credibility through the IBL ecosystem; and a Port Louis / Mer Rouge operating location that sits inside real maritime infrastructure rather than a purely tourism-facing setup. None of that guarantees strong economics, but it is harder to copy than a pretty brochure and a Facebook page.

Industry Trends and Tailwinds

  • Mauritius remains a tourism-driven island economy, which supports leisure marine demand but also makes activity vulnerable to shocks and seasonality.
  • Private charter and excursion markets are crowded, so differentiation often collapses into service quality, network access, and reliability.
  • Asset utilisation is everything in marine leisure. Idle boats destroy returns quietly and relentlessly.
  • Customers increasingly expect strong digital discovery and booking convenience; this entity’s public presence appears thin.
  • Group adjacency to a shipyard and repair platform can reduce downtime and maintenance friction if managed well.
  • If the company is primarily a strategic vehicle rather than a true growth business, commercial discipline may lag because nobody really “owns” the outcome.
Positioning gap
The company’s declared business is clear, but its external market identity is weak. That usually means one of two things: either most business comes via relationships and group channels, or the entity has never been forced to behave like a properly managed commercial brand.

EOS Suitability Assessment

EOS is most effective where leadership wants traction, clarity, accountability, and operating rhythm. This company is small enough for EOS discipline, but the real gating factor is whether anyone truly wants to manage it as a business rather than merely preserve it as a legal and strategic vehicle.

Recommendations

Eight recommendations: four financial / operational and four EOS-specific. Sequence matters. First stabilise truth and ownership, then install rhythm, then push improvement.

Financial and Operational Recommendations

Recommendation 01

Reconcile payables immediately

The CFO / finance lead should produce a line-by-line ageing of the MUR 54,235,907 trade and other payables within two weeks, separating genuine suppliers, intercompany balances, accrued expenses, and any stale items. Success means management can explain exactly what that liability stack is, not wave hands at it.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 02

Define the economic role of the entity

The board and operating leadership should decide whether CNOI Investissements is meant to be a profit centre, strategic leisure adjunct, asset-holding vehicle, or commercial brand. Success means one explicit purpose statement and one named owner for financial outcomes.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 03

Attack receivable days

Receivables at MUR 9,217,416 imply roughly 127 DSO days. Put a weekly collections cadence in place with named account ownership, disputed-balance log, and collection target. Success = DSO pushed below 75 days within one quarter.

Within 30–60 Days
Recommendation 04

Measure asset utilisation properly

For a marine-leisure business, asset utilisation is oxygen. Track boat availability, booking rate, trip count, gross profit per trip, downtime, and cancellation reasons. Success means management can tell whether the assets are underused or just poorly monetised.

Within 30–60 Days

Overall Assessment — Conclusion

The Bottom Line

CNOI Investissements looks less like a strong standalone business and more like a strategically parked subsidiary with decent gross economics but poor financial structure. The opportunity is not “grow fast.” The opportunity is to force clarity: why it exists, who owns it, and whether the numbers justify its current model.
