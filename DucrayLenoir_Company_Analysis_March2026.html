Executive Summary

A nearly 70-year-old healthcare and agro-chemicals distributor generating MUR 1.05 billion in revenue — but whose core trading business is structurally loss-making without MUR 66.8M of "Other Income" that it cannot explain in one line of its P&L.

Ducray Lenoir Limited (C971, BRN C07000971) is one of Mauritius's most established medical equipment, pharmaceutical, and agro-chemical distributors. Incorporated in 1956 to serve the sugar industry, the company pivoted into healthcare in the early 1990s under Dominique Galea's leadership, adding pharmaceuticals and veterinary products at the turn of the millennium. Today it represents a diverse portfolio of international brands — including Philips medical imaging — and holds trusted supplier status with the Ministry of Health & Wellness, the Ministry of Agro-Industry, most private hospitals, and a growing network of African markets through its subsidiary Medical International (Madagascar) and operations in Seychelles, Comoros, Rwanda, Eritrea, and South Sudan.

The company is 88.9% owned by Kasa Holdings Ltd, itself wholly owned by the Kasa Group — the family conglomerate of Dominique Galea (HEC, born 1952), who acquired Ducray Lenoir in 1988 and has since built a Mauritius-spanning group that includes Rey & Lenferna, La Prudence Leasing, and several manufacturing entities. A meaningful group restructuring in 2021 transferred Ducray Lenoir from KASA Investments Ltd to the newly incorporated Kasa Holdings Ltd. December 2024 saw a board refresh: Stéphanie de La Hogue (Galea's daughter, who manages the family corporate office) became Chairman, and two new directors were appointed — signalling a generational handover in governance oversight.

Financially, FY2024 tells two stories simultaneously. The headline is impressive: MUR 1.05B turnover, MUR 28.6M PAT, 11.9% ROE, and an E&Y-audited balance sheet. The underlying story is more concerning: gross profit of MUR 165M is entirely consumed by admin costs of MUR 171.7M plus finance costs of MUR 27.6M, producing a trading loss of MUR 34.3M. The company is rescued only by MUR 66.8M of "Other Income" — whose nature (management fees, investment returns, government rebates?) is undisclosed in the CBRIS filing. Simultaneously, MUR 404.7M sits in trade receivables (140-day DSO), cash is MUR 7.9M (0.8% of revenue), and a new floating charge of MUR 131.8M was filed in August 2025 — post-balance sheet — suggesting FY2025 liquidity pressure has already materialised into formal debt. EOS suitability sits at 5.8/10, conditional on access to the MD and clarity on the Dec 2024 governance transition.

Key Finding — The Other Income Problem
Ducray Lenoir's core healthcare and agro-chemicals trading business generated a MUR 34.3M operating loss in FY2024 (GP MUR 165M minus admin MUR 171.7M minus finance costs MUR 27.6M). Profitability is entirely dependent on MUR 66.8M of "Other Income" — a figure representing 6.3% of revenue and 234% of PAT — whose source is not disclosed in the CBRIS filing. If that income is intercompany transfers, it is subject to group decisions outside management's control. If it is government rebates or tender bonuses, it is lumpy and non-recurring. Until this line is understood and stress-tested, any EOS or strategic conversation with this company must treat the MUR 28.6M PAT as fragile. The August 2025 floating charge of MUR 131.8M — filed after a balance sheet showing zero borrowings — confirms that FY2025 is already under meaningful pressure.

Strategic Note — Group Context and 2021 Restructuring
In 2021, the Galea family restructured the group: Kasa Holdings Ltd was created to hold all medical and services assets, separating them from the property business (KASA Properties Ltd). In early 2022, Ducray Lenoir Ltd was transferred from KASA Investments Ltd to KHL. This restructuring — combined with the December 2024 board refresh — suggests the group is actively preparing for a generational handover. Dominique Galea (born 1952, now 73) appears to be transitioning operational governance to the next generation via Stéphanie de La Hogue's appointment as Chairman. Understanding this succession dynamic is critical: EOS would accelerate the handover by building the structural permanence that protects the business from founder-dependency.

Products and Services Portfolio

Division 1 — Medical Equipment and Healthcare Technology: Sole distributor for Philips Healthcare imaging products in Mauritius (including the Philips Azurion 7 Cathlab and Philips Big Bore RT CT scanner recently installed at C-Care Darné and the New Cancer Hospital). Also distributes other diagnostic, surgical, and laboratory equipment to government hospitals, private clinics, and educational institutions. This is the highest-value, longest-cycle, relationship-intensive segment — sales are project-based and capital in nature, explaining both the high DSO and the lumpy revenue profile.

Division 2 — Pharmaceuticals and Veterinary Products: Wholesale and retail distribution of pharmaceutical goods through 19 Poivre Street (Port Louis). Registered for pharmaceutical retail and wholesale under CBRIS business activities. Serves Ministry of Health tenders and private pharmacy/clinic channels. Veterinary products added at the turn of the millennium. This segment likely generates the most consistent, recurring revenue.

Division 3 — Agro-Chemicals and Pesticides: Dealer and retail seller of pesticides, herbicides, fertilisers, and listed chemicals (Cantin Dr Yves Street, Mauritius). Serves Ministry of Agro-Industry tenders and the agricultural sector. This segment leverages the same regulatory relationships and supply-chain infrastructure as the pharmaceutical division. The large warehouse at Camp Benoit likely supports both pharma and agro-chemical storage.

Geographic Presence and International Reach

Ducray Lenoir operates from two primary locations in Mauritius: 19 De Poivre Street (Port Louis, HQ, registered office, pharma and medical) and 20 Black River Road, Camp Benoit, Petite Rivière (showroom, warehouse >100m²). Internationally, the company operates through Medical International (subsidiary, Madagascar) and Ducray Lenoir International (sister company under KHL) which covers Seychelles, Comoros, Rwanda, Eritrea, and South Sudan. This multi-country footprint — across six African markets — represents a genuine competitive differentiator and a significant growth platform, but also introduces foreign exchange exposure, credit risk concentration, and operational complexity that a 62–80 person head-office team must manage.

Charge Register — Financing History

Seven charges are registered against Ducray Lenoir's movable and immovable property. The earlier charges (1998–2005) — totalling approximately MUR 33.3M in floating charges plus a MUR 975K charge — represent legacy banking facilities likely established when the company was smaller and revenue was in the tens of millions. These charges have not been discharged from the register, which is common in Mauritius even after facilities are repaid, and should not be over-interpreted unless the amounts remain outstanding.

Post-Balance Sheet Financing — A Red Flag Requiring Explanation
Two recent charges demand attention: (1) A USD 765,000 floating charge (equivalent ~MUR 34M) filed in October 2024, likely representing import trade finance or an international credit line established for the African subsidiaries. (2) A MUR 131,765,000 floating charge filed 12 September 2025 — eight months after the FY2024 balance sheet showed zero formal borrowings. A MUR 131.8M new floating charge on a company with MUR 7.9M cash and MUR 28.6M PAT strongly suggests FY2025 liquidity deteriorated materially, forcing the company to convert trade credit pressure into formal secured debt. This is the most significant negative signal in this entire report. Any engagement with Ducray Lenoir in 2026 should begin by understanding what drove this charge.

The 2 Numbers That Matter Most
1. DSO 140 days (MUR 404.7M in receivables): If Ducray Lenoir could compress DSO from 140 to 90 days — still above Mauritius benchmark but realistic for government-heavy B2B — it would release approximately MUR 144M in cash. That is equivalent to 5× its current cash holdings, eliminates the need for the 2025 floating charge, and fundamentally changes the company's strategic options. Structural Competitive Advantages

1. Government trusted-supplier status across three ministries: Having earned and maintained approval from the Ministry of Health & Wellness and the Ministry of Agro-Industry over multiple decades is not easily replicable. Tender approval processes, product registration, and inspector relationships represent a multi-year investment that functions as a structural entry barrier. This is Ducray Lenoir's most defensible competitive asset.

2. Philips Healthcare exclusivity (if maintained): The Philips medical imaging distribution relationship — evidenced by recent installations of the Azurion 7 Cathlab at C-Care Darné and the Big Bore RT CT scanner at the new Cancer Hospital — is the highest-value commercial relationship in the portfolio. Any distributor with exclusive rights to Philips medical imaging in a small-island healthcare market has a semi-monopoly position for major hospital procurement. The risk is that Philips could reconfigure its regional distribution strategy.

3. Multi-country African platform: The combination of Medical International (Madagascar subsidiary) and Ducray Lenoir International (DLI, covering Seychelles, Comoros, Rwanda, Eritrea, South Sudan) creates a multi-country distribution footprint that very few Mauritius-based companies of this size can match. This African presence is a genuine growth platform — and likely a major contributor to the MUR 66.8M Other Income (through intercompany fees or dividends from profitable African operations).

Industry Trends and Tailwinds / Headwinds

  • Tailwind — New Cancer Hospital infrastructure spend: The Mauritius government's new dedicated cancer centre represents a multi-year capex programme that directly benefits Ducray Lenoir as the Philips imaging distributor. The RT CT scanner installation is already confirmed. Further radiotherapy and diagnostic imaging procurement is likely.
  • Tailwind — Private healthcare growth in Mauritius: C-Care Group (formerly Clinique Darné) and other private hospital operators are expanding clinical capabilities. Major equipment investment decisions flow through specialist distributors like Ducray Lenoir.
  • Tailwind — African healthcare access investment: International and government funding into healthcare infrastructure across sub-Saharan Africa creates a long-term growth runway for medical equipment distribution. Ducray Lenoir's existing African platform positions it ahead of later entrants.
  • Headwind — Government procurement delays and DSO: Mauritius government healthcare procurement is characterised by slow payment cycles — structurally producing the 140-day DSO visible in FY2024. Without policy change, this problem does not resolve itself.
  • Headwind — Sugar industry contraction: Mauritius sugar output has declined materially over the past two decades, directly shrinking the agro-chemicals market that gave Ducray Lenoir its original foothold. This division faces structural volume pressure unless the company pivots to fruit, vegetable, and food-security agriculture.
  • Risk — Philips distribution agreement concentration: If Philips Healthcare reorganises its Indian Ocean distribution (as global OEMs increasingly do in response to digital health market changes), Ducray Lenoir's most profitable segment faces existential disruption. The company appears to have no disclosed mitigation for this single-partner dependency.
  • Risk — Currency exposure from African operations: Operations in Madagascar, Rwanda, South Sudan, and Eritrea involve currencies with variable stability. The USD 765,000 floating charge (Sept 2024) may reflect exposure management for the African business.

Each dimension rated 1–10 with evidence from public data, financial analysis, and CBRIS records. The December 2024 governance transition is the key EOS timing signal for this company.

01 — Organisational Size and Complexity 7 / 10

At 62–80 employees and MUR 1.05B revenue, Ducray Lenoir sits at the upper end of the EOS sweet spot. More importantly, its organisational complexity exceeds its headcount: three distinct business divisions (medical equipment, pharma, agro-chemicals), an international subsidiary (Madagascar), a sister-company African network (6 countries), government tender management, and a 2021 group restructuring all create the coordination and accountability challenges that EOS is specifically designed to resolve. Highest-Priority EOS Tools for Ducray Lenoir

Accountability Chart

The most urgent tool. Three divisions, a subsidiary, a sister-company network, an MD, a COO, and a new Chairman — with no publicly documented ownership structure below the board. Before any other EOS tool lands, the company needs a single page that answers: who runs what, who reports to whom, and what does each seat own? The dual MD/family-Chairman structure makes this both urgent and politically sensitive.

Scorecard & Measurables

A 140-day DSO visible in the annual accounts is a management information failure — the leadership team should be seeing DSO, cash, payables, and gross margin by division every week, not every year. Building a 13-week rolling Scorecard with 5–15 weekly measurables — DSO trend, cash position, payables ageing, division GP — would be the single fastest way to surface the receivables crisis and assign ownership to fix it.

V/TO (Vision / Traction Organizer)

The December 2024 board transition is the natural trigger for a written V/TO. The new Chairman needs to lead a process that answers: where is Ducray Lenoir going in 10 years? What does 3 years look like (African market share? New Cancer Hospital as anchor client? Digital service platform?)? What are the 3–7 Rocks for 2026? Without this, Betchoo's "transformative healthcare" aspiration remains a LinkedIn tagline rather than a company direction.

Rocks (Quarterly Priorities)

The company has at least four mission-critical priorities competing simultaneously: resolving the working capital crisis (DSO programme), renegotiating or managing the MUR 131.8M 2025 debt, integrating the post-2021 group restructuring operationally, and growing the African platform. EOS Rocks force the leadership team to choose the 3–7 most important things for the next 90 days — preventing the paralysis of doing everything poorly at once.

Issues List and IDS

The receivables crisis, the Other Income opacity, the admin-exceeds-GP structural problem, the Philips agreement concentration — these are Issues in EOS terms. They are sitting in the business unresolved because no formal Issues List and IDS (Identify, Discuss, Solve) discipline compels the leadership team to confront them weekly. The Level 10 meeting's Issues List section would have forced a DSO conversation months before it became a MUR 131M charge.

Level 10 Meeting

The most structurally impactful EOS tool for a company whose divisions, geographies, and ownership layers all create information silos. A weekly 90-minute Level 10 Meeting attended by the leadership team (MD, COO, division heads) would create the cadence that is visibly absent from the financial signals. Meeting discipline is the lowest-scoring EOS dimension here precisely because no visible rhythm exists — making it the highest-leverage starting point after the Accountability Chart.

Recommendations

Eight recommendations in total — four financial/operational (addressing the structural vulnerabilities before or in parallel with EOS) and four EOS-specific (sequenced for the December 2024 governance transition window). Financial stabilisation is prerequisite to EOS uptake: no leadership team installs a new operating system while managing an active liquidity crisis.

Financial and Operational Recommendations

Recommendation 01

Diagnose and Disclose the "Other Income" Line

The MUR 66.8M Other Income is the single most important unknown in this report. Before any EOS engagement or strategic conversation proceeds, the MD must be able to explain — to the new Chairman, to any EOS implementer, and to potential lenders — what this income consists of, how recurring it is, and whether it is subject to group-level decisions. If it is intercompany management fees, a written intercompany agreement should exist. If it is tender rebates, the pipeline should be modelled. If it is investment income on the MUR 186.6M NCA "Others," those underlying assets need to be disclosed and valued. Assign Clyde Anodin (Head of Accounting) to produce a one-page Other Income breakdown by category within 30 days. This is not optional — it is the starting point for every other decision.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 02

Launch a Structured DSO Reduction Programme — Target 90 Days in 12 Months

The 140-day DSO is not a market problem — it is a process problem. A 50-day reduction (from 140 to 90) releases MUR 144M in cash. The programme must be three-pronged: (1) segment receivables by client type (Ministry of Health, private hospitals, African subsidiaries) and assign named ownership for each segment; (2) negotiate milestone payment structures for large medical equipment projects (Philips installations) rather than single invoice at delivery; (3) introduce a weekly cash collections dashboard as part of the Scorecard. Success metric: DSO below 100 days by December 2026, below 90 by June 2027. Owner: CFO (or COO Gianni Apollon if no CFO). No EOS programme will succeed if the leadership team is managing a cash crisis every week — this must be stabilised first.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 03

Investigate and Explain the August 2025 MUR 131.8M Floating Charge

A MUR 131.8M secured charge filed against a company that showed zero borrowings six months earlier demands an explanation. In the first EOS conversation with Dharmen Betchoo, this needs to be understood: What was the facility used for? Is it a revolving trade finance line or a term loan? What are the covenants? Does the new Chairman (De La Hogue) understand it fully? The charge was filed against movable and immovable properties — meaning the company's physical assets are now encumbered. This constrains future financing flexibility and must be factored into any 3-year picture. If the charge was required to fund working capital because receivables ballooned further in 2025, it confirms the DSO programme in Rec 02 is urgent.

Immediate Priority — Before EOS
Recommendation 04

Close the Admin-to-GP Gap: Margin Recovery or Cost Restructure

Admin costs exceeding gross profit (MUR 171.7M vs MUR 165M GP) is a structural issue that has two solutions — and likely requires both. Margin side: Review the pricing structure for government tender contracts in pharma and agro-chemicals; if the company is consistently winning on price and sacrificing margin to maintain volume, that model is loss-making at scale. The medical equipment division (Philips) should be delivering 20–25% GP — investigate why the blended margin is only 15.7%. Cost side: MUR 171.7M admin in a 62–80 person company = MUR 2.1–2.8M per head fully loaded. Identify the 3–4 largest cost categories within admin (likely sales overhead, vehicle fleet, premises, and intercompany charges) and set a 15% reduction target over 18 months. Success metric: admin costs below MUR 155M by FY2026, GP-to-admin ratio positive by FY2027.

The Bottom Line

Ducray Lenoir is a MUR 1 billion distributor with a 70-year track record, genuine government relationships, and a Philips Healthcare partnership that most competitors cannot match. It is also a company whose core trading business is structurally loss-making, whose cash position is existentially thin, and whose leadership is navigating a generational governance transition while simultaneously managing a working capital crisis. Clarify ownership before committing to a full engagement.
